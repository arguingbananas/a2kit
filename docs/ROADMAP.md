## 1. Find out why the Apple II crashes when idle

**Shelved on 10 November**, with the machine reliable enough to develop on: with extra cooling it ran clean for 48 hours (below). Keep the extra cooling in place. The tests to pick up later are listed under *When this is picked up again*.

The machine has crashed or hung eleven times with the receiver waiting and nobody touching it:

Two earlier oddities may be related, but that's unproven: a first picture at 115200 that came up as lo-res blocks with a dead keyboard, and a drop to Applesoft with nothing pressed.

**8 November: the canary found a failing RAM chip.** With the RAMCard removed, free RAM (`$0B00–$BFFF`) held its pattern for 8 h 42 min. Then, from 08:32 until the run was stopped at 10:09, bit 0 of about half the bytes in `$8000–$BFFF` read wrong at every check, in both directions, with different bytes each time. Every listed change was bit 0 alone (xor `01`), and nothing below `$8000` changed, the stack page included. On a II+ each 16K bank is one row of eight 4116 chips, one per data bit, so this points at the bit-0 chip of the `$8000–$BFFF` row, or its socket. It appeared only after hours of running, so heat may bring it on.

**8–10 November: 48 hours clean with extra cooling.** With nothing else changed (the same chip, the RAMCard still out), the canary ran from 22:01 on 8 November to 22:02 on 10 November with better cooling on the Apple II: 565 checks, every one with 0 bytes changed, and no stack wipe, crash or silence. That is more than five times as long as the bit-0 errors took to appear without it (8 h 42 min), and over twice the longest run before any earlier failure (22 h 40 min). So heat is the likely trigger. What it heats is still open: the bit-0 chip itself, or the power supply, whose aging capacitors could let the RAM's supplies sag or ripple as it warms, enough for one weak chip to fail. A sagging supply would also explain the stack wipes in the `$0000` bank better than one bad chip does.

**What the memory dumps show.** Dumps taken after the last three were compared with dumps of the same machine when healthy, and with the reset replayed on its own ROM in the simulator:
- **Memory changes with no program writing it.** The stack page was wiped to zero three times, where a healthy dump shows it full of power-up noise. Booting, `ramload` and an Applesoft cold start don't do this. A failing DRAM chip can lose its contents with no write at all, so this doesn't need a stray write.
- **The changes outside the stack sit in `$8000–$85xx`:** `$8080`, `$80C6`, `$80DF` and `$80FF` changed in both dumps that cover them. That's the bank the canary later caught failing, which fits a bad chip there better than the address fault (a glitch on A15 or in selecting the 16K RAM banks) first suspected. No a2kit code writes there.
- **The receiver's code was never damaged**, and `$D000–$FFFF` always read as ROM, so the RAMCard was never switched in.
- **A wiped stack would itself crash the receiver** the next time its listening routine returns. In the simulator, overwriting its stack did exactly that. So the stray writes may cause the crashes rather than just follow them, though not every crash fits.

**Ruled out so far:**
- **Closing the serial port.** The long hangs happened with the port open. Two quick jumps did follow a close, but only after a Monitor `980G` had left interrupts on: the card's line change raised an interrupt, and the junk IRQ vector sent the CPU to `$0000`. The receiver now keeps interrupts off, and a 30-minute test closing the port 360 times then passed.
- **An NMI going straight to the crash address:** `$3FB` holds junk that would have changed the registers.
- **Reseating the cards and pigtail:** the machine hung again 5 h 23 min later.
- **The RAMCard as the only cause:** the canary run had the card removed and still found the failing bank. Apple-Cillin II's memory test had passed with the card fitted, but on a cool machine; the fault took hours to appear.

**The failing bit is in the language card's socket.** Rows C, D and E of the motherboard's RAM hold `$0000–$3FFF`, `$4000–$7FFF` and `$8000–$BFFF`, and the leftmost chip of each row, column 3, holds bit 0. So bit 0 of `$8000–$BFFF` is chip **E3**, and E3 is the socket a language card's pigtail plugs into: its chip is pulled so the cable can take its place. That matches this machine, whose pigtail goes into the leftmost socket of row E (photographed 10 November). These come from several Apple II forum answers that agree, not from Apple's own documentation ([RAM layout](https://www.applefritter.com/node/33651), [E3 and the language card](https://www.applefritter.com/comment/77315), [Gayler, *The Apple II Circuit Description*](https://www.applefritter.com/files/2023/10/26/W.%20Gayler%20-%20The%20Apple%20II%20Circuit%20Description.pdf)). E3 had been opened and refitted several times: fitting the RAMCard, reseating everything, and taking the card out for the canary run.

**11 November: the stack wipes are back, and fast.** With the RAMCard out, a plain chip in E3 and the extra cooling on, the stack page was wiped to zero 23 minutes into a canary run, and the receiver crashed as a result. That is the symptom of 6 and 7 November, in the `$0000–$3FFF` bank (row C), not the bit-0 errors in `$8000–$BFFF` (row E) that the canary found on 8 November: the 45 KB it watches, E3's bank included, didn't change. It came after 23 minutes where the same cooling had held for 48 hours, so either something changed since 10 November (the RAMCard refitted and removed again, E3 reopened twice) or the fault isn't heat alone. The stack wipes now look like the main fault, and the power supply the leading suspect: a sagging or noisy supply can make DRAM lose its contents with no write, in any bank.

**11–14 November: clean again since.** Same machine and setup (RAMCard out, plain chip in E3, extra cooling on), from a fresh `ramload` and later the new boot disk: a canary run from 16:34 to 17:34 changed nothing; another from 18:57 on 11 November to 06:57 on the 12th ran its full 12 hours (about 345 checks) with no change in free RAM, no stack wipe, crash or silence; and the receiver then sat idle until the 14th and still answered, with no crash recorded. So the 23-minute wipe is the odd one out, which fits an intermittent fault (such as a marginal supply) better than a steadily failing one. It doesn't clear the machine: the next steps below still stand, starting with the supply measurements.

**15 November: four days held, and a full test run.** A read-only look before anything was sent found the 45 KB of free RAM, E3's bank included, still holding the pattern written at 18:57 on the 11th, apart from where a show had been loaded since, and the stack page intact: four days without a change. Then `ping`, the ROM reads, a picture, the starter show, a video with sound, the Dylan clip and `lcprobe` all ran on the machine (results in the [Installation Guide](INSTALL.md#tested-setup)), followed by a clean 15-minute canary run.

**15–16 November: the inverted pattern, then a day of activity, both clean.** Same setup. From 19:43 on the 15th to 07:43 on the 16th, the canary ran with every bit of its pattern flipped (a small script outside the repo, checked in MAME first): about 350 checks, every one with 0 bytes changed, and no stack wipe, crash or silence. With the 11 November night, every bit of `$0B00–$BFFF`, E3's bank included, has now held both a 0 and a 1 for 12 hours without changing, so a bit stuck at one value is ruled out there. Straight after, from 07:44 to 19:44, the machine was kept busy instead of idle: every 15 minutes it played the starter show and the bouncing ball with sound (both uploaded with `--install`), ran `ping` with its speed test, and checked the crash record, with the receiver checked every minute in between. All 47 rounds passed, the receiver matched this computer's build throughout, and uploads held steady at 8,729–8,833 bytes/s. That makes 24 hours in a row without a fault, idle and busy, but the stack wipes have come and gone before, so the supply measurements below still come first.

**22–24 November: a stack wipe with the RAMCard refitted, then 19 hours clean with a new card.** The Microsoft RAMCard went back in for the language-card video tests (slot 0, its cable in E3). The first canary run stopped answering after about 40 seconds; on the second, bit 0 of 855 bytes in `$8000–$BFFF` read wrong within 4 seconds, and 1,298 a few minutes later: E3's bit, through the card's cable. A third run was clean for 45 minutes, and `lcprobe` and the card video worked. Left idle afterwards, the machine crashed between about 17:40 and 19:14 with two `BRK`s at `$0000`, and the dump showed the stack page wiped again while main RAM, the ROM and the receiver were intact: the signature of 11 November, which came with the card out. On 23 November the RAMCard was swapped for a Microtek 16K card (slot 0, cable in E3). `lcprobe` reported 16K GOOD, a 45-minute canary and the card video were clean, and a canary from 21:45 on the 23rd to 17:00 on the 24th (19 h 15 min, about 230 checks) changed nothing, with no stack wipe, crash or silence. So the Microsoft card's cable looks like the cause of the bit-0 errors, but the stack wipes are still unexplained, and the supply measurements below still come first.

**There may be two faults at once:** a marginal supply and one weak chip. The two symptoms sit in different places: bit 0 alone in `$8000–$BFFF` (one chip, E3) after hours of warm-up, and whole-page stack wipes in `$0100–$01FF` (another bank). One bad chip explains the first but not the second; a bad supply can explain both, but would usually hit more than one bit. They can also feed each other: a slightly low or noisy supply often shows up first in the weakest chip, which would explain why cooling seemed to cure everything for 48 hours and then didn't.

**Next, in order:**
1. **Measure the supplies**, the one check that could explain both: on a 4116, pin 8 is +12 V, pin 9 +5 V and pin 1 −5 V, with pin 16 ground. Measure on a row C chip and on E3, cold and after an hour. More than about 5% off, or drift as it warms, points at the supply; aging capacitors are a common fault at this age, fixed with a recap kit or a replacement supply.
2. **If the supply is bad, fix it and run the canary again.** Bit-0 errors that remain in `$8000–$BFFF` are then the chip alone.
3. **Settle the chip:** swap E3 with E4. If the errors move to bit 1 (xor `02`), it's the chip; if they stay on bit 0, the socket. 4116s are cheap, so replacing E3 outright is a reasonable shortcut.
4. Keep running the canary between steps, to see whether the stack wipes come back as fast.

**The earlier theory (8–10 November), still possible for the bit-0 errors:** heat as the trigger, through a marginal contact in E3, from the socket's springs or the chip's legs worked loose by the refitting, or later the pigtail's own connection, that opens as the board warms. It fits one bit in one bank, the hours of warm-up before it failed, and the cure by better cooling. Otherwise: the E3 chip itself, or the power supply. A loose E3 contact doesn't obviously explain the stack wipes, which are in the `$0000–$3FFF` bank, unless the failing chip disturbs the shared data bus; a sagging supply would explain both.

**11 November: a weak spot found.** The IRQ vector at `$03FE–$03FF` still held `$FFFF`, its power-on contents. Any interrupt that got through would send the CPU to `$FFFF` and on into zero page, which fits the second crash. The receiver keeps interrupts off, but the first crash's stacked `P` shows they were on by then. Pointing `$03FE` at the crash handler would report such a jump instead of letting it run wild (see [Small improvements](#3-small-improvements)).

**Restarting after a hang without losing the evidence:** don't switch off. Press Ctrl-Reset, then type `CALL -151`. The first Reset after power-up runs an Applesoft cold start, which zeros `$0800–$0802`, so type `800:78 A9 00` to put the receiver's first bytes back. Type `48:04` so `G` starts with interrupts off, then `980G`, and dump memory before anything else.

**When this is picked up again**, changing one thing at a time:
1. **The reverse test:** run `a2kit canary` without the extra cooling. If the bit-0 errors come back after about 9 hours, heat is confirmed.
2. **Chip or power supply:**
   - Heat only the suspect chip with a hair dryer on low for a few minutes, with `a2kit canary --check 30`. Failing within minutes, with the rest of the machine cool, points at the chip.
   - Or cool only the power supply, then only the motherboard, and see which run stays clean.
   - Measure the supplies at a RAM chip, cold and again after about 9 hours: on a 4116, pin 1 is –5 V, pin 8 +12 V and pin 9 +5 V, with pin 16 ground (check against a datasheet first). Drift beyond about ±5% points at the supply; ripple needs an oscilloscope.
3. **The chip swap:** with the RAMCard out, swap the E3 chip with its neighbour E4, never into the `$0000–$3FFF` row, which holds the receiver. If the errors move to bit 1 (xor `02`), it's the chip; if they stay on bit 0, the E3 socket. With the RAMCard in, E3 is reached through the pigtail, so a bit-0 failure then points at the pigtail's connection or the E3 chip. A chip that fails because of the supply would still move, so this doesn't clear the supply on its own.
4. If it still fails with good RAM and a good supply: a standalone Apple II memory tester with no serial and no a2kit running, then the plain `]` prompt test (the cursor blinks even if the CPU has stopped, so type a key now and then).

**While developing:** if anything odd happens (a hang, garbage on screen, a dead keyboard), don't power off. `a2kit crash`, `peek` and `canary` can capture the evidence first, using the restart procedure above.

## 3. Small improvements

- **Bytes the receiver can give back.** It is 362 of the 380 bytes the boot disk has. If new receiver code needs more room, the cheapest saving is the centred banner: dropping its two lines (`lda #17` / `sta CH`) frees 4 bytes and puts `A2KIT` back at the left.
- **Check the video player is running before streaming without `--install`.** `show send` now checks its sequencer (see Done), but a running video player can't answer memory reads, so `stream` without `--install` still trusts that the player is there. A read request answered by the receiver would show it isn't; check first that the player safely ignores such a request.
- **Update the Installation Guide for ADTPro.** ADTPro 2.1.0 works on this machine at 115200 by booting its disk image from the Floppy Emu. Note that it needs the language card; that a2kit and ADTPro can't share the port; and that a `.DSK` on the Emu shouldn't be formatted in-system (write to a blank image, or use the Emu's `Blank.WOZ`).
- **Document the working cable.** A photo or pinout of the cable and the card's jumper setting is still missing.
- **Correct pixel shape.** Circles come out about 9% narrow on a 4:3 screen. Add an `--aspect` option, on by default once checked on a real monitor.
- **Add a gamma setting** to the converters, if pictures look too dark or too bright on a real monitor.
- **An ordered dither for colour pictures.** `image --colour` offers Atkinson, which spreads each area's error into the next, and `threshold`, which spreads none. Shades between the six colours (photos, gradients) come out as an irregular grain with Atkinson and as hard bands with `threshold`. A fixed pattern, as black and white video already uses (`--dither ordered`), would mix them more evenly; compare the three on the colour TV. The palette bit makes it harder than in black and white: each group of 7 dots has only two colours besides black and white.
- **Compress uploads:** done for pictures (see Done). Sound barely packs (zlib manages only 60–90% on the examples), and a dithered picture packs to only about 55–75%, because what's left would need entropy coding, too slow to unpack on a 1 MHz 6502.
- **Reduce the sound's whine.** Measured on the Apple II+: notes in tune, no page-change tick, but a whine at the 7,972 Hz sample rate about 5 dB below the notes. Try a faster player, such as 64 cycles a sample (about 16 kHz) with 3-bit samples, and compare by ear. A measured pulse-width table could also even out the loudness steps.
- **Calibrate video timing if needed.** `BURST_MARGIN` in `stream.py` and the 95% frame budget in `videoconv.py` have held up so far, including the stress clip. Adjust them only if a real clip runs late.

## 4. Bigger features

- **Shows that run without the computer.** Save a show to a disk that boots and plays it. This needs a loader beyond track 0: a small custom one, or a distributable ProDOS 8 (check its licence).
- **More pictures per show:** done (see Done). Still open: keeping pictures in a language card for even more of them, and unpacking during a `hold` so the swap isn't delayed.
- **Colour pictures:** done for still pictures and video (see Done). The colours have been checked through a composite video grabber and by eye on a colour TV (see Done).
- **Video without tearing:** done, with page flipping, flips timed to the vertical blank, fast blocks and preloading (see Done). Still open: try it on a IIe, which also has a vertical-blank bit at `$C019` that could make the wait shorter; with `--refill` (memory reused during play), the iris wipe of `hello_apple.gif` still holds a picture for up to 0.5 s and the first wipe 0.3 s, the others 0.1–0.2 s. The iris isn't short of memory: the 6502 can't draw its busiest pictures from memory inside a frame, so it needs a faster draw or a picture spread differently. `--refill` still needs a check on the II+ with the grabber, and could then become the default with `--preload`.
- **The effects library:** page flipping, mid-screen mode changes, raster-style effects and scrolling text. The II+ has no vertical-blank signal, so timing would use the floating bus.
- **Mockingboard support.** The biggest sound upgrade: the card plays music by itself, so the CPU stops being the sound source. See [the design](#mockingboard-design) below.
- **Two-way messages.** Memory reads add a first Apple II to computer channel. Going further would allow keyboard input, status reports and interactive uses such as a speech-to-text thin client.
- **A stronger checksum:** CRC-16 instead of XOR, if errors ever get through.

### Mockingboard design

*A plan, not yet started. The register-level details are from memory of the card's documentation; check them against it before writing code.*

**Why.** Speaker sound is 1-bit and cycle-timed, and has to be kept in RAM. That caps it at about 6.2 s (9.2 s with the language card), keeps the CPU busy, and brings the whine at the 7,972 Hz sample rate. Longer sound has to be sent in chunks, with a pause for each upload: a 93-second poem took 20 chunks, about 2.9 s apart. Dense music doesn't survive at all. A Hells Bells MIDI rendered for the speaker was muddy with the full band, and its 55–65 Hz bass was inaudible. On a Mockingboard the chips make the sound themselves, so none of these limits apply.

**The card.** A standard Mockingboard, usually in slot 4, has two AY-3-8910 sound chips. Each has three square-wave tone voices, a noise generator, a 4-bit volume per voice and a hardware envelope, so six voices in all, set through 14 registers per chip. The CPU reaches each AY through a 6522 VIA: a register number or value goes on the VIA's port A, then control lines on port B are pulsed, roughly 20–30 cycles per register write. The VIAs' timers can interrupt the CPU at 50 or 60 Hz to drive a player. Music is a few register changes per frame: at most about 700 bytes/s, and usually far less. Some versions add a speech chip, left out here.

**Who keeps time.** There are three ways to play, built in this order:

1. **Streamed, with the computer keeping time** (first). The computer sends each frame's register changes at the right moment, and a small player on the Apple II writes each to the chip as it arrives. There are no replies, just as with `a2kit stream` for video. It needs no interrupts, so the receiver's interrupts-off design and the junk IRQ vector at `$03FE` don't matter. At about 300 bytes/s it uses under 4% of the line. Music can be any length, with no gaps.
2. **Inside the video stream** (next). Video frames already carry up to 1,824 bytes at 115200 baud, and a few register writes per frame would give continuous music under video. Today the speaker's sound has to stop the picture for bursts; with this, sound effects could land on the bounces.
3. **From memory, timed by interrupts** (for shows that run without the computer). The tune is uploaded first, then a VIA timer interrupt writes the registers. This is how most Apple II music software works, and free RAM holds several minutes. The receiver can't take serial bytes while interrupts fire (at 115200 a byte arrives every 89 cycles), so uploads and playback can't overlap. The player must set its own IRQ vector and turn interrupts off again when it's done.

**Where the music comes from.**

| Source | Effort | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YM files | Low | Register dumps from the same chip (Atari ST, Amstrad); only the 2 MHz clock needs rescaling to the Mockingboard's 1.023 MHz. Thousands of chiptunes exist. |
| VGM files with AY-3-8910 data | Low to medium | The same idea, from a different family of machines. |
| MIDI | Medium to high | Needs arranging into six voices, with drums as noise and short envelopes. Hells Bells would fit, with its bass as a real tone: the AY plays down to about 16 Hz. |
| WAV | Not worth it | Stepping a volume register plays samples, but that is as CPU-heavy as the speaker and no better. |

**In a2kit.**

- **`a2kit mbprobe`:** finds the card, like `lcprobe`, by looking for a 6522 timer that counts down in each slot, and plays a test tone.
- **`a2kit music FILE --port …`:** converts and streams. `--preview out.wav` renders the register stream on the computer with a small AY model, so it can be heard and tested without the Apple II.
- **Shows:** a `music = "tune.ym"` scene, played from memory.
- **Video:** `a2kit video --music tune.ym`, as part of the stream.
- **The receiver doesn't change.** It has only 18 of the boot disk's 380 bytes to spare, so the players are separate uploaded programs, like `sequencer.bin` and `video.bin`.
- **Tests:** a VIA and AY model in the py65 simulator records register writes, so tests can check the right writes at the right times; the preview renderer turns them into sound. AppleWin and MAME emulate the card, for checks before real hardware.

**Steps.**

1. Detection and a test tone (`mbprobe`).
2. The streaming player and the YM converter, with the WAV preview.
3. Music inside video.
4. The MIDI converter.
5. The play-from-memory player for shows.

**Before starting.**

- **Hardware:** decide on the card, an original or a modern clone, since the model affects detection and whether a speech chip is present.
- **Timing:** fit it only after the idle crashes are understood. A new card is another device on the bus, and would cloud the canary results. The design, the simulator model and the converters can all be done without the card.

- **A guided hardware check.** A script that walks through the checklist below and records the results.

## Checklist for a new setup

Use this for any setup not yet listed as [tested](INSTALL.md#tested-setup), going from the smallest step to the largest so each one tests one new thing:

1. **Check the disk controller.** An early Apple II may have a 13-sector controller (boot ROM 341-0009) rather than the 16-sector one (341-0027) the boot disk needs. If so, type the receiver in with `./a2kit mon receiver.bin`.
2. **Free the serial port.** Stop anything else using it, such as the ADTPro server. A cable and jumper setting that work with ADTPro are a good sign for a2kit.
3. **Start the receiver.** Boot the disk, or type it in, and confirm the `A2KIT` banner.
4. **Check the link.** `./a2kit ping` should count to `A2KIT PING 10 OF 10` on the Apple II's bottom line and report `Link OK`. Note the port delay and the speed.
5. **Build up.** Send a full picture, then the starter show, then video without sound, then with sound.
6. **Leave it idle for a while.** Run `./a2kit crash --watch` for at least a couple of hours, once a build with it is available. Short tests missed the crashes in section 1.
7. **Write down the results:** what worked, any retries, real upload times, how the sound and pictures came across, and a photo of the jumper block and cable wiring.

**If something goes wrong:**

| Symptom | Likely cause or fix |
|---|---|
| `no ACK` with the banner showing | Cable, jumper orientation or handshake lines |
| Uploads slower than `show plan` estimates | `./a2kit ping` measures the real rate; try a different `--chunk` |
| Video overruns, late frames, or damage after sound bursts | See the video timing item in section 3 |
| Disk doesn't boot | 13-sector controller: type the receiver in or upgrade the PROMs. Otherwise, try the image in an emulator |
| A key doesn't stop a show or video | Adjust where the programs poll the keyboard |

## Done

- **First hardware test** (2 November 2026, Apple II+). Everything worked after two fixes: a show ending on text page 2, and banners piling up on the booted receiver's screen.
- **`./a2kit ping`:** checks the link, measures the port's delay and the real upload rate, and names the kind of fault.
- **Built-in serial ports:** documented, and part of the tested setup.
- **A USB-to-RS-232 adapter** (8 November 2026, an FTDI FT232R, on `feature/crash-reporter`). On its default 16 ms latency timer, uploads ran at about 8,000 bytes/s with 128- or 255-byte packets, because each packet took a whole number of 16 ms periods. With the timer at 1 ms they ran at 8,546 and 9,119 bytes/s, against 8,743 and 9,157 on the built-in port. Every a2kit command now sets the 1 ms timer itself on Linux, with no `sudo`, and `ping` judges how much a slow port costs from its speed test. A picture, the starter show and all four videos, with and without sound, then ran cleanly over the adapter, looking and sounding the same as on the built-in port.
- **115200 baud** (3 November 2026). Now the default, with `a2kit-boot-19200.dsk` as the fallback. Ping, a picture, the show and the video all ran, including a stress clip at the full byte budget with and without sound.
- **Measured results:** in the [Installation Guide](INSTALL.md#tested-setup).
- **Version control:** the project is on GitHub.
- **Restarting after Ctrl-Reset** (11 November 2026): the Manual explains why `980G` crashes after the first Reset and how to repair it, and the troubleshooting table lists the symptom.
- **`show send` checks the sequencer** (11 November 2026): without `--install` it reads the sequencer back first, and uploads it if it's missing or different, so a show after a power cycle or a `canary` fill no longer jumps into whatever is at `$0C00`.
- **The receiver says who it is, and reports stray interrupts** (11 November 2026): the banner is just `A2KIT`, which freed 22 bytes. Every command reads the receiver back on first contact, says whether it's this computer's build and at which speed (on the Apple II's bottom line too, for uploads), and names the `--baud` to use when the speeds don't match. The IRQ vector now points at the Monitor's `BRK` path, so a stray interrupt is reported like a crash instead of running wild. The banner is centred, and the status line, on the line above `ping`'s, reads `VERSION MATCH` or `VERSION MISMATCH`. Tested on the Apple II+: an old receiver was reported as a mismatch, `ramload` and then the new boot disk from the SD card both matched, `$03FE` read `$FA4C`, and a show sent without `--install` after a canary fill uploaded its own sequencer and played.
- **Sound and video previews** (15 November 2026): `audio --preview out.wav` renders the speaker's exact clicks (tested against the real player in the simulator, cycle for cycle, and against MAME's recording: loudness correlation 0.94), and `video --preview out.gif` shows the screen as the player draws it, frame by frame (tested against the stream's decoded screens and the real player's final screen).
- **"Hello, Apple"** (18 November 2026): a new example animation, `examples/hello_apple.gif`, 32 seconds of original black and white shadow play drawn by `make_examples.py`: an apple that splits to show the title, a walker on a hill at sunset, birds that turn into falling leaves, with ink, wipe and iris transitions. At 10 frames a second and 115200 baud it averages 620 bytes a frame against a 1,094-byte budget; only the full-screen transitions fall behind, for about a second each. Played on the Apple II+, recorded through the video grabber.
- **Memory refilled during play** (24 November 2026, `video --refill`): once a preloaded block has been drawn its memory is free, and calm frames leave the line time to spare (about 99 KB over `hello_apple.gif`). When the memory is full, the encoder puts the next block where drawn ones were, choosing the place that can take the biggest block in time, and sends it beforehand in store ops (`$7F n c l h`, 2n bytes, a check byte) added to frames with spare time. The player writes them to main RAM, or to the card with ROM still read (`$C081` twice), and refuses any outside preload memory. `hello_apple.gif` at 10 frames a second: 310 of 319 pictures without a card (273 before), 311 with one (280); worst holds 0.3, 0.5, 0.1, 0.1, 0.2 s. In py65 on the host's schedule, the whole clip with the card had no overruns and all 310 flips showed whole frames.
- **Preload into a language card, and busy pictures over several frames** (22 November 2026), ready for the language card on order: `video --flip --preload --language-card` carries the preloaded blocks on from main RAM into the card's `$D000–$FEFF` (12 KB more), laid out like sound; `stream --language-card` uploads them, and the player switches the card in (`$C080`) only while it draws a block from it, then ROM back (`$C082`), also on every refusal path. It refuses an op into `$C000–$CFFF` or the vectors' page, and a card block whose bytes aren't in the card. Copies moved from `$E000` up, which clashed with the card, to `$2000–$3FFF`, the screen, which never holds a block's bytes. A picture so busy that drawing it from memory takes longer than a frame (some wipe frames change 2,800–3,700 bytes) is now spread over up to six frames with a block in each, the same share of memory a frame, the smallest that finishes it in time. And `--preload-share benefit` weighs long busy stretches in pieces of at most 16 frames, so the iris wipe no longer loses to the split next to it. `hello_apple.gif` at 10 frames a second, sum of (hold − 1)² over the clip (lower is smoother): 137 before (277 pictures), now 132 (280) without the card and 80 (286) with it. In py65, on the host's schedule, the whole clip with the card had no overruns and all 286 flips showed whole frames. MAME isn't a test for flipped video: its serial link garbles even `main`'s stream, which plays cleanly on the II+.
- **Less stutter in flipped video** (22 November 2026): on the II+ the tearing was gone, but the full-screen wipes of `hello_apple.gif` stuttered, holding a picture for up to 0.8 s. They change 1,300–1,700 bytes a frame, and the line carries about 1,094, twice over with page flipping. Three changes. Fast blocks draw at 13 cycles a byte and 32 an entry (19 and 55 before) in a new format, with jump offsets into fixed code on pages of their own (`$1600–$18FF`) so the timing never changes. A page two pictures behind first copies from the page on screen the bytes that page already has right (2 bytes of the line a stretch, in the block after the mark or in mid-frame blocks). And `--preload-share benefit` (the default) plans the clip without preload, finds the stretches where pictures are held, and gives the memory to those that gain the most per byte (gain: the drop in the sum of (hold − 1)² over the stretch); `first` keeps the old first come, first served, with leftover copies on the line. `hello_apple.gif` at 10 frames a second: 259 pictures without preload, 277 with `benefit`, 288 with `first` (275 before). On the II+ through the grabber, every capture of both was within 0.9% of a real frame; the longest holds were 0.8 s in the cloud and last wipes with `first`, and 0.7 s in the iris and last wipes with `benefit`, whose middle and cloud wipes ran at the full 10 frames a second. The user found `benefit` slightly better (fewer choppy transitions).
- **Fast blocks and preloading for flipped video** (19 November 2026): the 20 ms the line stayed quiet at every flip, for the vertical-blank wait, is used now. After a mark that changes page the computer sends a *fast block*, 218 bytes (19 ms of the line) in a format of its own (skip, length, screen bytes), which the player stores while it waits, then draws from memory about four times faster than the line after the switch; the line is quiet only while it's drawn, about 5 ms. `--preload` goes further: when a picture can't be finished in its own frame, the encoder moves the smallest part of its work that lets it be on time into a block that `stream --install` uploads to free memory first (`$6000–$BFFF` after any sound), and an op, `$7F l c h`, draws it mid-frame. The host's quiet times come from a cycle-exact model of the drawing code (checked against the player for every block; the drawing code sits alone on a page so its timing can't change). Planning first showed that preloading everything would need about 89 KB, not the 7–18 KB first estimated (that assumed the 6502 could take bytes at any rate), so the 24 KB is used for the busy moments in order. In `hello_apple.gif` at 10 frames a second: 248 of 319 pictures with fast blocks alone (239 before), 275 with preload (35 frames, 24 KB, 3 s more to upload). On the II+ through the grabber: the apple split showed 23 of its 24 frames, up from 7, and every capture during playback but one (just after a jump in a wipe) was within 0.5% of a real frame; the split's worst was 0.07%. The first hardware run showed damage that the simulator hadn't: a restart in the wait loop right after storing a byte left 89 cycles between polls of the serial card, against a byte every 88.6, so now and then a block lost a byte and was drawn shifted. The loop now polls first in each pass (at most 67 cycles apart, a test checks it), each block ends in `$A5` so a damaged one isn't drawn, and something always follows a block so a damaged one can't swallow the next frame's mark. The host kept its schedule to within 0.25 ms on all 623 pieces.
- **Even pacing for flipped video** (18 November 2026): with whole frames only, busy moments went up late and unevenly: a picture appeared when its page happened to be finished, up to 7 frames late. Now, at each flip, the encoder plans the next picture. It takes the first frame ahead whose remaining bytes, checked by a dry run of the encoder, fit in the frames' time up to its own turn, so every picture appears when it's due. A page finished early waits for its turn. In `hello_apple.gif` that shows more pictures (239 of 319, up from 229), with none late in the apple split and wipes and at most 2 frames late anywhere. Letting the step only shrink by one frame at a time looked steadier on paper but showed far fewer pictures (196), so it was dropped. On the II+ through the grabber: the worst timing drift when a picture appears went from 633 ms to 67 ms (as fine as a 30 frames-a-second capture can tell), and 27 pictures appeared during the split and wipe instead of 21. Every picture was still a whole frame of the clip.
- **Only whole frames on screen** (18 November 2026): with timed flips, the apple splitting in two at the start of `examples/hello_apple.gif` still looked torn on the II+. Its halves change about 1,300 bytes more than each frame could carry, so the pages being flipped to were patchworks of old and new positions. Now `video --flip` switches to a page only once it's complete. While a page catches up, the next frames keep drawing it under a mark for the same page, which the player no longer treats as a flip (it used to show the unfinished page). Those frames need no pause for the vertical blank, so they get the full 1,094 bytes. The source frames that go by meanwhile are skipped: 90 of 319 in `hello_apple.gif`, in the split and the wipes, while the walker scene plays in full. On the II+, recorded through the grabber and compared with the clip's real frames: during the split, captures were 5.8% off the nearest real frame with timed flips alone (worst 22.9%), and 0.02% off with whole frames (worst 0.27%). No capture during the video was over 1% off.
- **Flips timed to the vertical blank** (18 November 2026): with `video --flip`, the player now switches pages only during the vertical blank, so a flip never lands mid-screen. The II+ has no vertical-blank signal, so it reads the floating bus, the byte the video circuit last fetched. Probes in MAME and on the II+ showed that in hi-res the circuit fetches the screen holes (the 8 unshown bytes at the end of each 128) only during the top third of the screen and the blank, never during lines 64–191, and the two machines agreed. So the player keeps `$9B` in the holes, waits until about six lines go by without it, and flips at the next one. A one-line test wasn't enough: it sometimes flipped up to 2 lines after the blank. From 16 starting points the flip landed on lines 192–241 in MAME and 192–238 on the II+ (the blank is 192–261). The wait takes up to one refresh, so the host keeps 20 ms of each flipped frame quiet: 875 bytes a frame instead of 1,094 at 10 frames a second. The wait gives up in time without a floating bus (tested in the simulator). Checked on the II+ through the video grabber with a clip of white and black screens at 7 frames a second, which makes untimed flips land all over the screen: over about 8 seconds of flashes, the untimed player showed 15 torn frames and the timed one none. `hello_apple.gif` played through too, and the receiver came back.
- **Page flipping for video** (18 November 2026): `video --flip` draws each frame on the hidden hi-res page and switches to it when the frame is complete, so tearing (seen in `examples/hello_apple.gif` on the II+) goes. A new frame mark, `$FB`, starts a frame on page 2 (short skips shrank to 1–122 to free it), and each frame mark shows the page the frame before it was drawn on, so older streams play as before. The player's checks now keep each frame inside its own page, and it clears both pages when it starts. On `hello_apple.gif` at 10 frames a second, flipping costs 777 bytes a frame instead of 622. The player keeps up with bytes 71 cycles apart (69 for a flipped stream; 115200 baud delivers one every 88.6). Tested in the simulator: the real player at 19200 and 115200 never wrote to the page on screen, every frame mark after the first flipped, page 1 was back on when it returned, and 24 random faults each recovered by the next frame. On the Apple II+ at 115200, recorded through the video grabber, `hello_apple.gif` played through twice and the receiver came back. Compared with the nearest complete frame of the preview, a typical captured frame was 0.04% of pixels off (0.16% without flipping), and the worst 1% were 2.4% off (4.5% without). The worst flipped captures were whole frames, not half-drawn ones. Watched on the monitor too: good. The wipes still fill in over several frames, now as whole pictures, and the flip itself isn't timed to the screen yet.
- **Colour video** (18 November 2026): `video --colour` converts each frame in the six hi-res colours. The stream's data bytes stay below `$80`, so the palette bit travels as state: two former skip codes, `$FC` and `$FD`, turn it off and on for the bytes that follow, and each frame starts with it off, so black and white streams are unchanged. The player ORs it into each byte, 3 cycles more, and still keeps up with bytes 69 cycles apart (115200 baud delivers one every 88.6). New example `examples/balloon.gif`: the colour landscape's balloon drifting across the sky, 16 s at 10 frames a second, about 400 bytes a frame. Tested in the simulator (the real player, a colour clip back to back at 115200: every palette bit landed, no overruns) and on the Apple II+, recorded through the video grabber: it played in colour, as the preview shows, and the receiver came back.
- **Character ROM rebuilt from the screen** (17 November 2026), for MAME, without reading the chip. Photos of the monitor (15 November) failed: the CRT's glow swelled every dot. A USB composite video grabber worked instead, in mixed mode, since it shows nothing for a full text screen (a II+ sends no colour burst in text mode). At about 2 capture pixels per dot, single dots come through partly as colour, so hi-res screens of random dot patterns, laid out like the text, calibrated how each pattern looks, including how much neighbouring scanlines blend in. All 256 screen codes read cleanly, and the glyphs laid out four times, with bit 7 set in the `$40–$7F` and `$C0–$FF` quarters, match MAME's checksum `64f415c6` exactly: the file is byte-for-byte the real `341-0036`. `a2kit chargen capture` and `decode` do it all (Manual, section 9); the ROM itself is never committed. Tests run the whole path on a simulated grabber with made-up characters.
- **More pictures per show** (17 November 2026): a show's third and later pictures are kept packed from `$6000`, alongside the sound, and a new sequencer step (`LOAD`) runs the unpacker at `$0300` to put each onto the page that isn't on screen just before it shows. The starter show ends on the colour landscape. Tested in the simulator with five pictures and sound, each checked on its page as it is shown, and in MAME and on the Apple II+: the new starter show uploaded in 3.2 s at 115200 baud, played, and the colour landscape read back from page 1 byte for byte, with no crash.
- **Packed pictures** (16 November 2026): `send` and `show send` upload a picture packed when that's quicker, and a 155-byte unpacker at `$0300` (`runtime/unpack.s`) unpacks it from `$6000` in a session of its own; the receiver is unchanged.
